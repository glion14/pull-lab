Small Dinner Playbook

Nov 10, 2026 · @Rob

## The short version

A (very extroverted) founder runs small, informal, cross-industry dinners for senior people (VP and up, mostly heads of strategy, business development and R&D). The dinner exists to give the guests something valuable: a room of peers from *other* industries they can talk to openly, with no competitors present. She never pitches at the table. She waits two weeks, then follows up personally and books a separate call. About 80% of dinner guests become clients, and others become advisors or send referrals.

Her one-line summary of why it works: lots of people host dinners, but she keeps hers deliberately informal, and that informality is the thing guests notice and thank her for.

## Her numbers

* **Dinner size:** 12–15 people, every time.
* **Outreach funnel per dinner:** she contacts about 100 people → about 30 reply → about 12 attend. (Messaging below)
* **Once someone replies, conversion is very high.** The only common reason for not coming is "I'm busy" or travelling, and those people usually ask to be told about the next one.
* **Dinner → client:** about 80% of guests become clients. (This is quite high.)
* **Lead time:** she invites about a month out ("a month to three weeks").
* **Effect on another startup:** when her investor's portfolio company copied her approach and stopped selling at the table, its conversion rate rose by about 50%.
* For comparison, Rob said a good cold LinkedIn sequence gets roughly an 8–10% meeting rate; he put her dinners at around 20%.
* **She runs a dinner in a new city in Europe every 2 weeks because it works for her.**

## Step 1: Set up the dinner

* **Pick a city and lock in a date before inviting anyone.** She started in London and now runs them across European cities (she mentions Munich, Amsterdam, and Philadelphia as places she invites people to). The date is set when the invitations go

out, so when people ask for details she can give them a specific date straight away.

* **Book a private room in a restaurant.** A small, closed room so people feel comfortable talking openly.
* **Keep it to 12–15 people.** Her reason: "I need to be able to talk to everyone." The small size is a design choice, not a constraint.
* **No Luma page, no formal invitation flow, no "mega guest list."** She contrasted this with her investor, who first ran a formal Luma event and got a very low response rate.

## Step 2: Build and curate the guest list

* **Who she invites:** people at the same level, VP and above. For her business that means heads of strategy, business development, or R&D.
* **Curate as you go rather than blasting 100 invites at once.** She sends invites in waves and adjusts the list as people say yes:
  * First she locks in 3–4 "core" guests.
  * For her, the goal is that nobody has a competitor in the room. (This is relevant to her situation, but probably not to yours)

## Step 3: The invitation (LinkedIn, sent through Lemlist)

She builds the list, loads it into **Lemlist**, and sends a very informal LinkedIn message. Her first message is roughly:

> I love your profile, we have [these people] in common, I'd love to meet you. I'm in [Munich / Amsterdam / Philadelphia], would you like to come to one of my dinners?

### Key features:

* **It's vague on purpose.** It doesn't explain the dinner or the company. Rob checked his read of it with her: reach out with a vague message first, and people almost always reply with a question like "can you send me more info?" or "why do you do these dinners?" She agreed: most of them do.
* **It's personal and casual**, pointing to mutual connections, not a sales pitch.

## Step 4: The follow-up when they ask "why?"

When they reply asking for more, her answer runs roughly like this:

We love bringing together people who think about innovation from different industries in different ways. You probably hit roadblocks in your job every week where you don't know who to talk to, and you can't talk about them at a conference because everyone there is a competitor. So why not come to this dinner with people [from other industries]?

Then she adds **social proof: a list of past attendees** by title and company (she named the head of R&D at Unilever and someone senior at McLaren as examples). She said this was harder at the start, but now the list is strong, and it makes people feel "if these people come to these dinners, it makes me special."

Then she gives the date.

## Step 5: People who say no

* People who reply but can't come (busy, travelling) go on a **"next event" list**. She treats their reply as a sign they're engaged.
* She also **turns the no into a conversation**: something like "That's a shame you couldn't come, I would have loved to meet you anyway." She said that's often how sales calls start even when they never come to a dinner.

## Step 6: Prepare for every guest

* Before the dinner she **researches every guest on LinkedIn using AI agents** ("tell me what they posted about").
* She goes in with **a conversation topic ready for each person**.

## Step 7: Run the dinner

* **Start with drinks before sitting down.** She uses this time to spot the quieter people.
* **Seat the shy people next to her** so she can draw them into conversation.
* **Do not pitch. No going around the table saying what everyone does.** She called this really important. The point of the dinner is to get guests to meet each other and to give them something valuable.

* **When guests ask "What do you do? Why did you invite us?"** (they generally do), she says something like: *we can take it offline, and I can tell you more about what we do in a separate call.* She doesn't answer it at the table.
* She leans on being a naturally social host. Some of her dinners now have their own WhatsApp group chats afterwards.

## Step 8: Right after the dinner: share contacts

* **At the end of the dinner she asks everyone if she can share their contact details with the rest of the table.** She called this "the big draw."
* She then sends **one group email with everyone's email address and LinkedIn.**
* Guests reply-all to it, and the replies are typically some version of: "this was so refreshing, I get invited to a lot of these but they're so formal; this was informal." She thinks she has found "a little informal niche."

## Step 9: Two weeks later: the personal follow-up and the call

* **She waits two weeks before any business follow-up.** She knows this looks like losing momentum; she does it on purpose so the dinner never feels like a sales event.
* The follow-up is personal: it was great, and it mentions **things they talked about at dinner** (she jokes, "your dog or whatever they talked about"). She says she writes down everything they talked about.
* She ties it back to the question they asked at the dinner: *you asked what we do; it'd be great to learn more about you and tell you more.*
* **Then she schedules a dedicated call.** That call is where the sales conversation happens.

## What comes out of it

* About 80% of guests become clients.
* Some become **advisors.**
* Some make **referrals** ("I know this friend you should meet").
* She also gets invited to things by guests (she mentioned being invited dancing, among other things), which tells you how social the relationships become.

## The rules she stresses most

1. **Don’t pitch at the dinner.** Not your company, not a round-the-table intro. Her investor’s portfolio company saw about 50% higher conversion just by stopping this.
2. **Keep it informal.** No Luma, no formal setup. Guests notice it immediately.
3. **Keep it small (12–15)** so the host can talk to everyone.
4. **Mix industries and keep competitors out.** Curate the list as acceptances come in.
5. **Same seniority level across the table** (VP+).
6. **Prepare a topic for every guest** and look after the shy ones.
7. **Wait two weeks** before following up about business.

## Related tactics she shared in the same session

### Cold calling, used only as a follow-up

* She doesn’t do “get a list and call 100 people.” Calls come **after** a LinkedIn message or email.
* **The more senior the person, the more likely they pick up,** in her experience.
* **Call early, around 8 a.m. their time:** they’re awake but not in meetings yet.
* **Match the language.** She’s currently closing a deal with a company doing about $4B in revenue: she had a Spanish-speaking teammate call the (Spanish) CEO directly, in Spanish.
* **The call doesn’t sell. It’s about the email.** Roughly: “I sent you an email, did you get it? If not, maybe the address bounced; let me check.” Or, about an opportunity: “We have a chance to buy the patents of a company in distress. I need two minutes. I’ve sent you the details; please read it, or tell me who the right person is, or forward it.”
* **Ask for as little as possible,** and **use a real deadline** (“the deadline is the 9th of November, look at it now, it’s in your email”). She says she isn’t a natural salesperson and doesn’t try to sell in three minutes on the phone.
* Rob called this a great point and showed a basic cold-call script built on the same idea (“Sent you an email about ___, does that ring a bell?”).
